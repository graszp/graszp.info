Definition

Nutrition is a practice in service of the norm of survival: the organism's seeking, choosing, and taking in of what metabolism needs. Metabolism happens; nutrition is done. It is the first practice, and the first place where an organism can get something right or wrong for itself.

Every other practical realm presupposes it. Foraging draws on sentience and, in most animals, on enaction; among social creatures food is shared, traded, and ritualized.

Example

A foraging bee leaves a thin patch of clover for a richer one. No rule tells it to, and nothing merely happens.

History Note

  • Aristotle: the nutritive soul is the first and most widely shared power of soul, present in every living thing, plants included (De Anima II.4, 415a23–26). Wikipedia: On the Soul
  • Hans Jonas calls the organism's freedom a "needful freedom": it must keep taking in matter to remain itself (The Phenomenon of Life, 1966).
  • Robert MacArthur and Eric Pianka found optimal foraging theory: animals choose food as if weighing energy gained against time and risk (1966). Wikipedia: Optimal foraging theory

Scope Note

Nutrition is not metabolism, though it answers the same need one rung up: metabolism is the pattern, nutrition the practice. Diet, cuisine, and the shared meal are later elaborations of it, in sociality and beyond.
