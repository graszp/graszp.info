Definition

Alētheia (ἀλήθεια) is the Greek word for truth. Formed from a- ("not") and the root of lēthē ("forgetting", "concealment"), it can be read as unforgetting or unconcealment.

Parmenides gave truth a goddess and a road; Plato set a plain of truth in heaven and a plain of forgetting in the underworld. Pilate's question, "What is truth?", has never had one answer, and Heidegger revived the etymology as the clue to what truth is.

History Note

  • Parmenides' goddess teaches "the unshaken heart of well-rounded truth" against the opinions of mortals (fr. B1.29).
  • Plato places a "plain of truth" (τὸ ἀληθείας πεδίον) above the heavens (Phaedrus 248b) and the "plain of Lēthē" in the myth of Er (Republic X, 621a).
  • Latin renders it veritas; Arabic ḥaqq and ḥaqīqa (truth, reality). Al-Ḥaqq, the Real or the True, is one of the names of God.
  • Heidegger reads alētheia as unconcealment (Unverborgenheit) (Being and Time §44, 1927). Robert Brandom's "alethic modal" vocabulary names necessity and possibility (Between Saying and Doing, 2008). Philip Pullman's alethiometer tells the truth (Northern Lights, 1995). Wikipedia: Aletheia

Scope Note

Alētheia is not a definition of truth, and Pilate's question is the Socratic question. In this nomenclature truth is a norm, the norm of explanation, and the etymology is suggestive in a different way: what is lost to forgetting is oubliation, and truth is in part what has not been forgotten.
